Before wearable phones, when Americans debated the right and wrong of a foreign war but not yet the possible end of democracy, Tres DeLuca picked up the extension in her bedroom at the wrong time. She recognized the voice but the tone was new, the unaccountable tremor. She tried to put the receiver back in its cradle like a newborn chick into its nest, but her hand was shaking. She heard the whispered response. “Hush, Harold.” Harold was her father. The whisperer was not her mother.

The next morning as always her brother went off to day camp, her parents to their high-end jobs. Robby picked her up as planned in the car he’d got for his birthday, and they drove to the city.

It was late August 1968, school to start soon but not yet. Tres and Robby lived in Highland Park, a place of intact families and clean, gently curving streets, where children rode their happy bicycles and nothing ever happened. In the city of Chicago, things were happening. The Democrats were smack dab in the middle of their convention. Lives rode upon it, or Robby’s did, a year away from his draft registration.

But today their concerns were more immediate. Robby had quit the tennis team for reasons he hadn’t shared. Tres’s father was having an affair or on the verge of one. And Robby’s dad had a downtown apartment where she and Robby were going. To have sex, the first time for both. Sex being in Chicago 1968 a locked room you were dared to break into. Where you would meet either your primal fear or an inconceivable joy, fountainhead of your personal power.

Practical matters were already in hand: the door key; rubbers. Next on their agenda was scoring a joint. They headed for Old Town, a likely source for what they termed (wittily, Tres thought) their needed refreshment.

The air was the golden blue of late afternoon, but Tres couldn’t see. Eyes burning, nose streaming, she kept her head down and held tight to Robby’s hand, and after an indeterminate span of steady pushing in a single direction they were out on the street. But it was bad there in a different way. Kids were trying to overturn a police car. Others were being clobbered. Someone shot photos till his camera was yanked away and smashed. He held up the pieces for all to see. A Black man in a white shirt and tie was trying to organize people. “Stay together,” he said into a bullhorn. “Hold the line!”

Someone yelled, “Get these fuckers off the street.”

Which fuckers? said Tres to herself.

Then the bullhorn was sailing. The man in the shirt tottered, trying to keep his feet while a cop laid into him. "Stop! He didn't do anything!" Tres couldn't hear her own voice. Where was Robby? Then she saw Robby with his trademark smile and the bullhorn in his hands. "Stay together! Hold the line!" he shouted. He was looking around for something, possibly the bullhorn's owner. Around them was a jam of people; hard to see what was happening. Then there was Robby on the qsphalt, trying to turn onto his stomach while a baton wielded blows. On his back; his precious head. "Don't you dare hurt him!" she screamed at the policeman, whose baton was flailing too fast to catch hold of. "Somebody do something!" Was that blood from his ear?

Every once in a while and for no fathomable reason, a person will break out of the character that seemed consigned from birth and do something unlikely. This is an insight Tres had once upon a time, and here it was again. Someone mocks you, and although it isn’t the first assault on the fragile shell of your self-love, a pair of atoms conjoin on the sun, a bolt of helium flares, a couple of neurons realign in your brain, and instead of dissolving in tears, your fingers curl into claws at the sight of the mean, pretty face of the girl at whose house you’d always wanted to sleep over. Without thinking, as if it were the most natural thing in the world, Tres pulled at the kicker’s arm. He shook her off, not roughly. “Watch it, girlie.” He bent forward to recommence. She felt an ineluctable, blind propulsion. What an early Christian, ecstatic in faith, might have felt in the coliseum as the lion charged.

Timing her jump for when the cop was bent over, she seized his collar while her foot found purchase on his holster belt. She was up on his back, her arms around his neck, legs around his waist. “Yee ha!” someone shouted. In support? The thought increased her strength. The cop bucked, then tried to unpeel her; she yanked back on his helmet. “Jesus Christ!” He gagged, then again took the Lord’s name in vain. A venial sin. She almost laughed. There were things worth dying for. She tightened her legs as to a horse, while her hands pulled so hard on his helmet he staggered back. Yee ha! She was riding piggyback. Riding a pig!

Then her blouse ripped, or her skirt, and she was lying on the street, under arrest, handcuffed, lugged to a van. Robby, a miracle, was already in there. “He said he’d die for me,” she told the cop hoisting her aboard. Her sleeve was ripped; her blouse hung from one shoulder. Robby smiled welcome. “You look kind of sexy!” But before she could respond—she had no idea how to respond and her arm was starting to hurt, a lot--he said, “Joke!”

And what could she say to that? When there was blood on his neck?

That night hippies and yippies would march to the Amphitheatre with plans to change the course of history. The kids from Highland Park would ride to the police station, from where Tres would call her parents, who took her to the hospital and spent their rage on the police who’d handcuff a girl with an obviously broken arm (“It’s just my wrist, Mom,” Tres would say as the cast was applied).

Months later, without a word from Tres, her mother would discover her husband’s affair and surprise everyone and herself by divorcing him, though none of their friends were divorced. She promised Tres to be a better mom than she had been a wife, and it turned out to be true, though the bar, Tres thought, was kind of low. Robby would tell her what he had dumbly confessed to Scully, on account of which Scully requested another doubles partner, to which Gallagher acceded when he learned why although Scully had sworn not to tell anyone. Tres, of course, would tell no one, because she loved Robby although he didn’t want from her what she wanted from him and never would.

But so? Once upon a time in Chicago as the sky darkened and humanity churned she rode a bad cop, hard like a jockey. The cop, his broad back between her thighs. And she, trembling on the brink of something so much larger than herself.
